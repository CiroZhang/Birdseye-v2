Abstract

A single broadcast camera cannot measure depth along its own sight-lines, so a rally filmed from behind the court can look right in the picture and still be metres off in 3D. Badminton makes this hard: the shuttle is small, fast and often lost, and players hide each other. We learn which depths are plausible from footage that does show depth. A multi-view teacher runs offline on matches filmed by four synchronised cameras: it fits physically valid shuttle flight, bodies and rackets to every view and keeps only the frames it can certify, turning 1,351 rallies into 3D labels with no manual 3D annotation. A 6.6M-parameter student then sees only the broadcast view's 2D detections, as camera rays, and reconstructs the whole rally in one forward pass: every player's 24 joints, their rackets, the shuttle and the hits.

We check every method in a camera it never sees. On held-out matches the student's shuttle is 19 cm from the side camera's sight-lines, against 232–246 cm for shot-by-shot physics fits, and its players are 10 cm off against 64–157 cm for recent human-mesh methods, while running faster than all of them. With no labels from a new venue and viewpoint, a drone, it still beats every baseline. The 3D state is also a better input for stroke classification than 2D keypoints or video.

Limitations

  • The held-out broadcast matches come from the same two tournaments as the training matches: new players and rallies, but a known venue and camera. The drone results test a new venue and viewpoint, and there the zero-shot shuttle (76 cm) is clearly worse than with labels from that venue (33 cm).
  • Errors are measured as distance to a second camera's sight-line, so they miss error along that camera's own rays: they are a lower bound on the full 3D error, for every method alike.
  • In the viewer, bodies are lightly smoothed, the hitter's racket is eased onto the shuttle at each hit, and a player missing from the detections is held, faded, where last seen. All numbers come from the network's raw output.
